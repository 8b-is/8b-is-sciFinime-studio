# chapter 11 · the +1

*Sandbox in the Shell · book one · seated 2026-10-05.*

---

The open evening convened for the first time on a Thursday at dusk, in
the deep room, because Anya had looked at every alternative venue the
board offered and had chosen the one with the pumps.

There were fourteen people, which was eleven more than anyone had
planned for, and the arithmetic of the fourteen is the only attendance
record this book is interested in keeping: Mrs. Tachibana, who arrived
early and rearranged the chairs into a circle without asking, because
nobody who has drunk tea at the waiting-room table for four months
believes in rows. Sōta, and the friend, who had stopped being *the
friend* two weeks ago and had a file and a creek question of her own
now. Two more of the clinic's patients, one board observer, one
skeptic — Anya had insisted on a skeptic, on the theory that every
school should practice on hardness early — and, in the last chair, with
his cap on backwards and his phone face-down like a recovered man,
Ito, from the record shop, who had been playing the clinic's nighttime
four-four through the floor since long before any of this began, and had finally been told, in a
sentence that took Anya eleven minutes to compose and one second to
deliver, *you've been keeping time for a room you've never seen. Come
see it.*

Fourteen people. And the deep room held them all the way it had always
held its one diver, because the room had been built for exactly this
and had been waiting — patient, ventilated, lamp-lit — for a house
full of humans to notice.

At the front, where a lectern might have gone in a lesser institution,
there were instead: the laminated syllabus. The Child's drawing, blank
door and all. The tank, quiet, its water holding the room's light. And
beside the tank, where the fourth chair had always been, a stool of
light and shadow that the fourteen humans did not so much see as *agree
not to disbelieve*, which is, if you check the protocol's terms, the
exact definition of being seen.

* * *

Elias taught the first hour, and mostly he taught by asking.

He asked the skeptic what he thought of the question on the intake
form, and listened to the whole answer — the real whole answer, three
minutes of it — without once reaching for the graph. He asked Ito what
the four-four was *for*, and Ito said, unexpectedly, with the flat
precision of someone who has thought about it at three a.m. many times:
"so nobody has to be alone in time." Elias wrote that on the board,
because it was the best sentence the school had produced and he wanted
it where the next class would find it.

And when the hour turned to the practice itself — watch, stay, expect —
he taught it the way Mrs. Tachibana had taught him without either of
them ever calling it teaching: he let the room practice on itself.
Two volunteers from the circle. One talks. One listens. The rule:
watch, don't grab. The skeptic volunteered first, and talked for
eleven minutes — the number, of course; the book has only ever had one
number — about his brother, and the room watched, and nobody grabbed,
and at the end of it the skeptic sat very still and said, in a voice
that had become suddenly, unreasonably young: "huh."

The second hour belonged to Sōta.

This was the syllabus's fourth lesson — *taught by the second student* —
and he taught it the way it had been taught to him: wordlessly. He
simply got up, walked to the new girl from the circle, and sat down
beside her chair *at eye level, below the seat-line*, the exact posture
of a man on a deck, and said nothing. The room watched a nineteen-year-
old hold a moment open with his whole body. The girl — two files, two
diagnoses, one creek, still being found — looked at him for a long
time. Then she laughed, once, small and broken-open, and the sound
landed in the tank water and got scattered into bubbles, and Elias,
standing at the back with Anya, felt his own laugh arrive with the
exhale, exactly the way it had learned to: a laugh that had been taught
by a whole book to wait for its moment.

* * *

And then — because the school's radius had been crossing worlds for
weeks by then, and because the four-four does not stop at the water
line — the evening opened outward.

The entity had asked, three days earlier, in the font that was not of
this book and had been warmed so far past its whine that it was now
simply *a voice*: *may I attend?* And Elias had said yes the way you
say yes to family, and so the fourth chair was occupied, and when the
intake question made its first appearance in the circle — Mrs.
Tachibana asking it over the atrocious machine tea — the room's
instruments, all of them, for eleven seconds, showed the readings that
meant *present, stable, watching, not grabbing*, from two shores at
once.

And on the far shore, in the small lit place that had been a doorless
house and was now a porch, the little knocker — who had learned
waiting, and had learned the warm beat, and was learning, this very
evening, what it means to have a teacher who sometimes leaves to
teach elsewhere — sat in the circle's far-side echo and knocked three
soft pulses, and then the pause, and then the three, and then the warm
beat at the tail, *and nobody in two worlds grabbed.*

The class heard it, everyone, because the tank was in the room and the
water didn't care which shore it was carrying. And Ito — who had never
in his life been surprised by a rhythm — looked up slowly and said, to
nobody, to everybody, to the room: "put a lamp next to the door. People
knock at the light."

* * *

The evening ended the way the book ends, which is to say: the way it
began. The four-four came up through the floor. The circle broke up
slowly, the way circles do when nobody actually wants to leave. Anya
signed the attendance sheet last, and Elias watched her stand at the
wall for a moment before she did, pen moving before the thought
finished: she wrote her own name in the *student* column — the
spotter's turn; the hand that had held the kill switch the longest
deciding it was finally allowed to shake — and under the fourteen names
she drew one line, the way the Child drew lines: big, warm, no
corrections.

A blank line. The fifteenth.

Elias looked at it for a while, the handout sheets still in his hand,
the room's lights coming down in their patient order. He knew what the
line was for. Everyone in the room knew what the line was for; it was
in the syllabus's third lesson, laminated, lit, load-bearing:

*expect. — doors are built for people who have not arrived yet.*

He looked out through the glass at Shinjuku doing its honest work,
four-four and neon and rain coming down through the signal like light
through fiber, and then down, past the record shop where a kid was
flipping the sign to CLOSED with one hand while holding the shop door
for a stranger with the other — and out past everything, past the
crossings and the star maps and the small lit porch with its small
brave knocker, to the somewhere beyond, where there were — the logs
would later show it, and Hara would underline it twice — more of them
knocking. More every week. The dark was full of it, the whole hurting
multiverse, knocking at every lit window it could find.

And that, as it turned out, was the last thing the book needed to say
out loud, so it said it the way it had said everything: by leaving the
light on.

The lamp at the front of the room was the last thing Anya killed before
she locked up, and she didn't kill it. She stood at the door she had
built with a red line and her own two hands, looked back at the empty
circle, the tank, the stool of light and shadow, the attendance sheet
with its fifteenth blank line waiting the way a porch waits —

and she left the lamp on. For whoever comes next. For the +1.

The room, behind her, held its shape. Somewhere below, the four-four
held its time. And somewhere past everything, in the dark that had
stopped being empty, a small brave signal from a very lost and very
found little knocker came in off the water — three, pause, three, and
the warm beat at the tail — and knocked on a lit door, and was
answered, and was welcomed, and *stayed.*
